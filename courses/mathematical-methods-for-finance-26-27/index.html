Welcome and learning agreement

The course is mathematically demanding, but no topic will be presented as a sequence of formulas to be memorised. Each subject will be developed through a stable sequence: intuition, definition, example, counterexample, theorem and exercise.

The complete programme remains unchanged. The large slide and exercise collections are reference libraries: the page will always distinguish core material, recommended practice and optional extensions, so that the expected workload is visible and manageable.

Course design

Teaching commitments

The course structure directly addresses preparation, clarity, workload, exercises, material availability, assessment transparency and access to support.

1 Prerequisites first

A diagnostic test, two bridge lessons and a complete revision package establish a common starting point.

2 Visible workload

Weekly work is divided into core, recommended and advanced exercises. The full banks are not compulsory homework.

3 Materials in advance

Lecture selections are published before class; tablet annotations and solutions follow after class.

4 Clear assessment

Duration, structure, scoring, permitted materials and representative questions are collected in one assessment sheet.

5 Exercises every lesson

The final 50-minute block is reserved for worked, guided and independent exercises.

6 Accessible support

Weekly office hours, email support, an updated FAQ and mid-course feedback provide routes for clarification.

Teaching method

How each three-hour lesson works

Every lesson runs from 14:30 to 17:30 and includes two planned 15-minute breaks. Starting and finishing times will be respected.

14:30–15:20 Concept and intuition

Motivation, prerequisite recall, diagrams, definitions and a first elementary example.

15-minute break
15:35–16:25 Theory and worked examples

Theorems, selected proofs, counterexamples and complete calculations written in an ordered form.

15-minute break
16:40–17:30 Exercise laboratory

One worked exercise, one guided exercise, one independent task and a short correction.

Tablet and annotated notes

Derivations and exercise solutions will be written on a tablet directly over the lecture slides. The annotated PDF will be uploaded after the lesson, avoiding fragmented notes spread across a traditional board.

Computational demonstrations

When numerical demonstrations are useful, they will use one consistent, free environment and ready-to-run notebooks. Programming syntax is not an examination objective.

Preparation

Prerequisites and mathematical bridge

The assumed background comes from undergraduate General Mathematics and Statistics. The course does not assume that every student remembers each topic fluently.

  • sets, functions, equations and elementary mathematical notation;
  • limits, continuity, derivatives, optimisation and basic integrals;
  • vectors, matrices, matrix multiplication and linear independence;
  • descriptive statistics, probability and random variables;
  • expectation, variance, covariance and conditional probability.
Before 23 September Non-graded diagnostic test with complete solutions and a topic-by-topic revision guide.
Lessons 1–2 Mathematical bridge: calculus, linear algebra, probability and notation.
During the course Short prerequisite recalls before each new topic and one-star bridge exercises.

Purpose of the diagnostic

The diagnostic is not graded and does not affect the examination. It identifies which revision route is most useful before the advanced modules begin.

Objectives

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

  • work with metric, normed, Banach and Hilbert spaces;
  • use measures, measurable functions, Lebesgue integration and convergence theorems;
  • analyse random variables, conditional expectations and modes of convergence;
  • use characteristic functions, the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem;
  • describe stochastic processes, filtrations, martingales, Markov processes and Brownian motion;
  • compute quadratic variations and stochastic integrals and apply Itô's formula;
  • use changes of measure, solve elementary SDEs and connect stochastic dynamics with PDEs;
  • present solutions clearly, verify hypotheses and interpret mathematical results.

Syllabus

Six-module course roadmap

The official programme is organised into six modules. A two-lesson prerequisite bridge precedes the modules, and the final lesson is devoted to consolidation and a complete mock examination. Topics from the completion package are integrated into their natural module rather than counted as an additional module.

Course structure Prerequisite bridge · 6 modules · final consolidation
Preparatory bridge Prerequisites and diagnostic review 2 lessons

23–24 September 2026

Topics: notation, elementary calculus, sequences, vectors, matrices, matrix multiplication, linear independence, probability, expectation and variance.

Purpose: establish a common starting point without removing any content from the official programme.

Module 1 Functional analysis 3 lessons

30 September, 1 and 7 October 2026

Core topics: metric spaces, convergence, completeness, Baire category, compactness, Heine–Borel, vector and normed spaces, Hölder and Minkowski inequalities, sequence and function spaces, Banach, Hölder and Hilbert spaces, orthogonality, projection, Bessel inequality and linear operators.

Integrated topics: smooth and Schwartz spaces, finite- versus infinite-dimensional reasoning and approximation.

Module 2 Measure, integration and Fourier analysis 3 lessons

8, 14 and 15 October 2026

Core topics: sigma-algebras, Borel sets, measures, measurable functions, Lebesgue integral, integrability, equivalence classes, monotone convergence, Fatou, dominated convergence, product measures, Fubini–Tonelli and Lp spaces.

Fourier topics: trigonometric polynomials, Fourier series and transform, Plancherel, convolution and FFT.

Module 3 Probability and conditional methods 3 lessons

21, 22 and 28 October 2026

Core topics: probability spaces, random variables, distributions, expectation, Jensen, variance, covariance, correlation, Cholesky decomposition, independence, conditional laws, characteristic functions, inversion, sequences of random variables, modes of convergence, Markov inequality, LLN, CLT and Lévy continuity.

Conditional methods: Radon–Nikodym, Bayes, conditional expectation, conditional marginals and Least Squares Monte Carlo.

Module 4 Stochastic processes and martingales 3 lessons

29 October, 4 and 5 November 2026

Core topics: process laws, finite-dimensional distributions, measurable processes, filtrations, martingales, Markov and Feller processes, Chapman–Kolmogorov, generators, backward and forward Kolmogorov equations, Brownian motion, Wiener space, Poisson processes and stopping times.

Martingale refinements: continuous, càdlàg, square integrable, local and uniformly integrable martingales.

Module 5 Stochastic integration and Itô calculus 3 lessons

11, 12 and 18 November 2026

Core topics: variation, Riemann–Stieltjes integration, semimartingales, Doob decomposition, quadratic variation, stochastic integrals of indicator and simple processes, extension in L2 and local L2, Itô processes and Itô's formula.

Extensions: continuous semimartingales, multidimensional calculus, integration by parts and Lévy characterisation.

Module 6 Measure changes, SDEs and Feynman–Kac 3 lessons

19, 25 and 26 November 2026

Core topics: changes of measure, density processes, exponential martingales, integrability, Girsanov theorem, Brownian martingale representation, stochastic differential equations and Feynman–Kac formulas.

Practice focus: identifying the correct measure, transforming drift, solving elementary SDEs and verifying the associated PDE.

Final consolidation Integrated revision and mock examination 1 lesson

2 December 2026

Programme map, complete mock written examination, correction, common-error clinic and final assessment guidance. No new topic is introduced in the final lesson.

Study planning

Workload and exercise policy

The complete slide decks and exercise collections are intentionally extensive. They provide coverage, alternative explanations and long-term exam practice; they are not all assigned at once.

Core Expected weekly work

Definitions highlighted in class and four representative exercises per lesson.

Recommended Consolidation

Four additional exercises selected to vary method and difficulty.

Optional Advanced practice

Two challenge problems, longer proofs and extension slides for deeper study.

The exercise banks are libraries

The 100-plus exercise packs are not weekly compulsory lists. A dated exercise sheet will identify the small subset expected for the next lesson.

Evaluation

Assessment and examination transparency

The final written examination is the confirmed assessment component. A single assessment sheet will collect all rules before the first lesson; no examination requirement will be communicated only orally.

Format Written examination
Question types Short theory, method selection and multi-step exercises
Programming No programming-language syntax is assessed
Detailed specification Published by 16 September 2026

The assessment sheet will state explicitly:

  • duration and total score;
  • number and type of questions;
  • score assigned to every part;
  • minimum requirements and passing rule;
  • permitted materials and calculator policy;
  • role and rules of any partial assessment;
  • criteria for mathematical correctness, reasoning and presentation.

Progress test and mock examination

A formative progress test will be provided after Module 3. The final lesson is reserved for a complete mock written examination and public correction. Any graded partial assessment will be announced, with complete rules, before teaching begins.
